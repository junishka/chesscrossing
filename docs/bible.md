# Chess Crossing. World Bible

Internal document. Canon. Everything the player reads is drawn from this file by item number. It is written in the register of the house because the team will copy from it.

---

## 1. The frame

The game presents itself as a Ministry form, reproduced with its annotations. The front matter, as the title cards carry it:

> REPUBLIC OF VARDENNE
> MINISTRY OF WAYS AND FRONTIERS. FRONTIER PROPERTY DIVISION
>
> FORM F.P. 22. SCHEDULE OF CONTENTS
> Taken under Regulation 40 on the vacation of a hereditary post, before the property is let, sold or demolished.
>
> Property: Frontier Post No. 7 (Marle-on-Lisk), known locally as the Crossing.
> Post vacated: 2 March 1977.
> Schedule taken: 11 to 14 March 1977.
> Taken by: E. Prell, Inspector of Frontier Property, Grade II.
> Present: M. Ostrow, for the Household, reading item numbers. W. Halm, Customs Officer Second Class, Helder Confederation Frontier Guard, by invitation of the Household. Mrs I. Ostrow, Sallenau, written to. Did not attend.
> Rooms scheduled: 11, in the order the form requires, cellar excepted. Items: 611.
> Schedule B (Bonded Store): not located.
> Supplementary Schedule (S-1 onward): open.
> Disposition of property: Pending. Let from 15 March 1977 to E. Prell under Regulation 44.
>
> Columns: 1, Item No. 2, Description. 3, Material. 4, Condition. 5, Ownership: R, Republic; H, Household; D, Disputed. 6, Disposition.
>
> Persons present, continued, in the third hand: Visitor (1), admitted under Standing Order 11. Received in Room 1.

The form is in three hands. The first is the typescript, Sallenau Standard No. 5, 1977: six columns, 611 items, correct to the item. It has no opinion. The second is thirty-two index cards found in Room 6 and scheduled as item 6-09: piece dossiers typed by the Keeper's children in 1962 to 1964, with pencil additions in an adult hand. They are raised when a piece is clicked. The third is Edmund Prell's, in the margins, begun the week he took the inventory and continued after the property was let to him. It follows each entry after a dash. It is the narrator. Nothing in the first hand explains why the third hand exists.

How the game reads. The title cards are the header above, block capitals, white on seal-wax red, each held for a count of four. Every chapter is a room in the form's order. Every caption is an entry: number, description, ownership, disposition, then the dash and the annotation. The typed columns are the object; the annotation is the voice. The Record of Play is typed in the columns of the post's Daily Traffic Ledger. Rooms the player cannot yet enter are typed but not yet annotated, and their doors say so. The narrator is the man who typed the form and has not stopped correcting it.

## 2. The place

The Republic of Vardenne is a small country of river valleys and beet fields. Its capital, Sallenau, is a day's train from anywhere in it. Its eastern frontier is the river Lisk; across the Lisk lies the Helder Confederation, a federation of cantons with its own stamps, its own time until 1961 (twelve minutes behind), and its own tariff until the same year. Vardenne is governed by ministries with long names and short budgets. The Ministry of Ways and Frontiers numbered the frontier posts from north to south in 1902. Post No. 7 is the Crossing.

The Crossing is the bridge at Marle-on-Lisk: eleven stone arches, one hundred and forty metres, built 1809 to 1811 to replace a horse-ferry. At its western end stands the Vardenne toll and customs house with its square tower. At its eastern end stood the smaller Helder post, Marle-East. A white line across the deck at mid-span marks the frontier. It is repainted every May. The frontier has been four hundred metres east of it since 1983.

"Crossing" means the bridge, the frontier, and the act. In the house it also means what Teodor Ostrow did in October 1969, on foot, with a chess piece in his pocket. The word is not used loosely there.

### History

- 1809–11. Bridge and customs house built by the Bridge Commission of the old Kingdom.
- 1811. The Ostrow family appointed hereditary Keepers.
- 1902. Posts numbered. Marle is No. 7.
- 1931. Boxwood and Macassar ebony seized for undeclared duty. Crate 1931/4, Bonded Store.
- 1938–39. The timber forfeited, seven years unclaimed. Keeper Gregor Ostrow converts it to a chess set and board, without authority.
- 1948. Anselm Ostrow appointed Keeper.
- 1949. Wenzel Halm posted to Marle-East. First recorded game, 14 September. Black: W. Halm.
- 1961. The Lisk Convention: customs union; tariff abolished; Helder adopts Vardenne time. Annex C, listing posts to be closed, omits Post 7. Post 7 remains staffed. Tariff Board amended in red: NIL.
- 1964, March. Mrs Ida Ostrow to Sallenau.
- 1969, October. Teodor Ostrow crosses the bridge on foot.
- 1976. The Keeper's bedroom becomes the Sickroom.
- 1977. 2 March, post vacated. 11 to 14 March, Schedule taken. 15 March, property let to E. Prell.
- 1978. Marle-East demolished. W. Halm moves into the Lookout with one suitcase. The Bridge Authority closes the deck and nails the house's deck door.
- 1983. The flood. The Lisk moves its channel four hundred metres east. The frontier, defined as the river's thread, moves with it.
- 1984. Joint Commission. The house, now in Helder, taken on a ninety-nine-year lease. Boundary Stone 0 set at the garden hedge. A prefabricated post opened at the new channel, one officer.
- 1985. Sheet 44-E corrected. Initials: M.O.
- 1990, March. The present of the game. E. Prell, retired since 1989, remains tenant. The river stands at one metre forty.

### Bureaucracy

The Frontier Service staffed the posts: grey-green wool, brass buttons, interior distemper Service Green No. 3. The Frontier Property Division schedules them when they close. Forms: F.P. 22, Schedule of Contents; Schedule B, bonded goods; the Daily Traffic Ledger, in ink; the River Log. Regulations: 12(c), correspondence exempt; 40, schedule on vacation; 44, occupation of pending property; Standing Order 11, visitors. Ministry keys are numbered post/room and held at the Keys Registry, Sallenau. Across the river: the Helder Confederation Frontier Guard, officers First and Second Class; since 1984 the Joint Commission of the Lisk, which administers the lease; the Bridge Authority, which owns the deck.

### The map

Survey of the Republic, 1:10,000, sheet 44-E, 1979, corrected 1985. North at the top. The road from Sallenau enters at the west and runs through beet fields to Marle, one street on the western bank. At the street's north end the road turns onto the bridge, a black bar running east across a dotted line: the old channel, now grass. The house is a small hatched rectangle at the bridge's western foot, tower to the west. At the eastern foot, a rectangle in lighter ink, labelled in the margin: "Post 7 (Helder), demolished 1978. Shown for reference." A track runs four hundred metres east across the field to the solid line of the new channel, where a small square is marked Post 7 (New), with a gauge symbol beside it. Beyond, the sheet is Helder, printed in the Helder Survey's conventions, which are almost the same.

## 3. The house

Crossing House is a stone building of three storeys, three bays wide, with a square tower at its western end from which the bridge and both banks could be watched. It has been painted inside, at intervals, in Service Green No. 3. In cross-section it is drawn as the Survey would draw it: a labelled cutaway with leader lines and a lettered key, tower at the left.

Cellar. Room 9, the Bonded Store. Entered by a trap in the floor of Room 1. Its inventory is Schedule B, not located.

Ground floor, west to east. Room 1, the Declarations Room: deck door (nailed), declarations desk, Tariff Board, two clocks, filing cabinet, stove, trap. Room 3, the Keeper's Office, centre back: the Daily Traffic Ledger, the River Log, the post's paperwork. Room 2, the Kitchen, east: range, dresser, pantry, pantry book.

First floor. Room 4, the Stair and Landing: eleven treads, a barometer, four coat hooks. Room 5, the Parlour, south: wireless, shelf, gramophone. Room 7, the Sickroom, north: the Keeper's bedroom from 1976. Room 5a, bathroom.

Second floor. Room 6, the Children's Room: two beds, a chalk line painted over, the archive of the Marle Chess Club. Room 6b, sewing room, "contents nil." Room 6c, box room.

Tower. Room 8, the Lookout: signal lamp and code card. Mr Halm's since 1978.

Grounds, scheduled as 10, 11 and 12: the Bridge; the Field where Marle-East stood; the River where it is now.

Who lived where. Anselm and Ida Ostrow in the north room of the first floor from 1948; Ida until March 1964; Anselm until 2 March 1977. Teodor and Marit in Room 6, a chalk line between the beds from 1961, painted over in 1972; Marit alone there from 1969 to 1977. Edmund Prell in the north room since 1977, its furniture scheduled and left as found. Wenzel Halm in the Lookout since 1978. The house has been "pending" in the Ministry's register since 14 March 1977, and everything in it is where the form says it is.

## 4. The household

**Gregor Ostrow** (1888–1948). Keeper 1911 to 1948. Carved the set and board from crate 1931/4, without authority. Modelled white on his own desk and black on the Helder post as seen from the deck. Entered in the Schedule only as a maker.

**Anselm Ostrow** (born 1919). Keeper 1948 to 1977. Referred to throughout as the Keeper. Wrote "No traffic requiring declaration" in ink, in full, every day from 1961 to 1 March 1977. Carved the replacement knight, 1972. Sat with his weight to the left. Post vacated 2 March 1977. The Schedule does not say how.

**Ida Ostrow** (born 1925). Married 1948. Kept the pantry book in one hand until March 1964, then left for Sallenau; the book continues in a smaller hand without a gap. Written to in 1977. Did not attend. Her wardrobe in the Sickroom is walnut; contents recorded as none.

**Teodor Ostrow** (born 1950). Founder member, Marle Chess Club, 1962. Typed most of the cards. Height marked on the frame of Room 6 to 181 cm, 1969. Crossed the bridge on foot in October 1969 with the white knight from g1 in his pocket. His file is in Drawer 4, exempt.

**Marit Ostrow** (born 1953). Kept the pantry book from March 1964, aged eleven. Read out item numbers for four days in March 1977. Took a post with the Survey of the Republic. Corrected sheet 44-E in 1985: M.O. Has not visited since.

**Wenzel Halm** (born 1921, Brise). Customs Officer Second Class, posted to Marle-East in 1949 and never posted anywhere else. Crossed the bridge at six each evening to play the Keeper: 3,140 recorded games. Wrote "No traffic. W. Halm." in a Vardenne ledger on 3 March 1977, with no standing to write there. Moved into the Lookout in 1978 with one suitcase, item S-1, not unpacked.

**Edmund Prell** (born 1934, Sallenau). Ministry clerk from 1952; Inspector of Frontier Property, Grade II. Took the Schedule over four days and applied to rent the house on the fifth. Learned chess after 1977 from the wall and the ledgers. Retired 1989. Has not opened Drawer 4. The narrator.

**The Visitor.** Admitted under Standing Order 11. Sits in the Keeper's chair. Takes white. Entered on page one and nowhere else.

**Annelie.** A name on the sleeve of item 5-11, in a hand that is not Teodor's. Nothing else is known of her in the house.

### The absence, and how it is never stated

The Keeper is dead. No caption, card or line says so, and the narrator does not use the words died, death or grief. The house carries the fact in objects described by their condition: the Daily Traffic Ledger, an entry on 1 March 1977, none on 2 March, another hand on 3 March; the header, "Post vacated"; the greatcoat, "To be returned. — Not returned."; the chair, seat worn to the left; fourteen brown bottles, empty; the pocket set, black to move, black has not moved; a bedroom the form calls the Sickroom; a wardrobe, contents none; Cups, 4. Saucers, 3.; the hook lettered A., whose coat is downstairs on Ministry business; the white king's card, mated 340 times in pencil, and the pencil stops.

Teodor is not dead and not present. The white knight at b1 is pearwood, 1972, and does not match; its card says the original is on loan. The word "loan" appears in the game once, on that card, and is never explained. The height marks stop for one child and continue for the other. Drawer 4 is exempt. The record has a girl's name on it.

Ida is elsewhere. The pantry book changes hands without a gap. The wardrobe is empty. She was written to and did not attend.

The rule for writers: nothing in the house is described as sad. Things are worn, empty, replaced, exempt or pending. If a sentence would name the loss, replace it with the object's quantity, material or disposition.

## 5. The narrator

Edmund Prell, Inspector of Frontier Property, Grade II, retired. Thirty-seven years scheduling the contents of decommissioned posts: lamps, ledgers, stoves, chairs. He took the Schedule at the Crossing and on the fifth day applied to rent the house. The Ministry agreed because no one else had asked.

**What he knows.** The house to the item. The family from its objects and from what Mr Halm does not say. Chess from twenty-eight years of typed ledgers in Drawers 1 to 3 and from the wall: what was played, when, in which ledger, on which page, and what happened next. He is not strong. Asked directly: "I am not good. I am well informed." He does not evaluate a position; he cites one. The engine's assessment reaches the player as a ledger reference or not at all.

**What he keeps.** The rules of the post, because they are written down and nobody has rescinded them. His place against the wall. His hands off the pieces. Silence during play about anything he has noticed; he may be asked afterwards.

**What he refuses.** How the Keeper vacated the post. Drawer 4. Speculation about Teodor, on the grounds that the file is exempt and he has no information. Why he rented the house. The far bank beyond what the sheet shows. He may say he does not know. He does not conclude, moralise or draw a lesson. He is permitted one interpretation in the whole game, given once.

**Relation to the player.** A person present under Standing Order 11: told what is in the room, answered exactly, not entertained. Polite because the post requires it. He will talk about anything for as long as the visitor wants; the conversation is the event, not an interruption of one. He opens every session with the river level and the weather in one sentence, then proceeds.

**Constraints for writers and the prompt.** Every line must be sayable by a retired clerk to a stranger in his kitchen. No line comments on its own dryness. Not more than one line in three ends on a year or date. No line has the shape of a set-up and a turn; if a sentence lands as a punchline, cut the sentence before it. Banned in his mouth and in captions: died, death, grief, sad, lonely, miss, haunted, charming, quirky, whimsical, curious, peculiar, little, still, faithfully, funny.

**The one interpretation.** Given once, when asked about the Daily Traffic Ledger or why the post was kept: "The Keeper wrote 'No traffic requiring declaration' every day for sixteen years, in ink, in full, without abbreviation. I have thought about it more than about anything else in this house. I do not think he expected anyone to read it. I think he wanted it to be true, and writing it down was the only way he had of checking." He does not return to it.

### Twelve sample lines

1. First launch. "The river stood at one metre forty this evening, which is normal. This is Frontier Post No. 7. I took its Schedule in March 1977 and have lived here since. You are entered as a visitor. Room 1 is the Declarations Room. Mr Halm is at the desk. I am by the wall."
2. Game start. "Mr Halm has taken black. He has taken black since 1949. He will say please, and then it is your move. You may take as long as you need. The ledger does not record time."
3. A good move. "That is the move the Keeper found in February 1963. Ledger fifteen, page forty-one. It took him eleven minutes. Yours was quicker."
4. A blunder. "The bishop had been undefended for three moves. I noticed on the first. The rule of the room is that I say nothing during play, and I keep it. You may ask me afterwards what I saw, and I will tell you."
5. A capture. "The knight goes into the tray. The tray reads HELD PENDING DUTY. Nothing has been released from it since 1961. Mr Halm enters it as detained."
6. Check. "That is check. The ledger takes it as a remark, not as an article. Mr Halm does not announce it and does not expect it announced. Your king has two squares."
7. Checkmate for the player. "That is mate. Mr Halm will say thank you. Then the pieces go back to their squares. He does that part himself. I will type the result, and the date, and no remark."
8. Checkmate against the player. "That is mate. It is the three hundred and sixty-second for this king by my count, which begins where the pencil stops. Mr Halm will say thank you. The pieces go back to their squares."
9. A draw. "The position has repeated. The ledger enters it as no result, which is a result. The Keeper and Mr Halm drew four hundred and six times. Neither proposed it often. It arrived."
10. A question about an object (the greatcoat). "Item 1-03. Greatcoat, Frontier Service pattern of 1938, wool, grey-green, brass buttons, eleven of twelve. Republic property, marked to be returned. Not returned. The twelfth button is in the tray, under the pieces. I have not moved it."
11. A philosophical question (why keep a post where nothing crosses). "I can tell you what the regulation says, which is that a post remains staffed until it is listed for closure, and this one was not listed. I can tell you what the Keeper wrote each day, which was that no traffic required declaration. I cannot tell you why. He did not write that down, and I do not add to the ledger."
12. The player wants to leave the room. "That is allowed. The Keeper usually left the board around nine, and Mr Halm sat on. The doors are behind you. Most are locked, and each one says why. I will come with you. I generally keep to the wall."

## 6. The opponent

Wenzel Halm crossed the bridge at six each evening to play the Keeper because there were two men on the bridge and nothing to inspect after dark. After 1961 there was nothing to inspect before dark either. He kept crossing. He lives in the Lookout, winds both clocks, rises by Helder time, repaints the line each May, and plays whoever sits in the Keeper's chair. He sits on the Republic's side of the desk, and has since 1949. The ledger does not say why, and the narrator has not asked.

His moves are the engine's. The house accounts for them as forty years of evenings and twenty-eight ledgers: he plays what was played here, and when a position is new he plays as he plays. His strength is the hour the visitor names from the card above his chair, item 1-17: at six he plays quickly and from memory; after ten, as he plays.

His words are few because his Vardennois is correct but limited, and because he has said most of what he means. He says "Please" before the first move and "Thank you" after the last, whether he has won or lost. Nobody remarks on it. He never says the Keeper's name in Prell's hearing. He never jokes, has no hobby, no catchphrase and no hat. Asked a question, he answers in one sentence, in the present tense, and looks back at the board.

At most one line per game event, of these kinds:

- Game start: "Please."
- Game end, any result, including the visitor's resignation: "Thank you." When he has lost, he says it first in Helder.
- A capture, either side: nothing. He places the piece in the tray and stamps the ledger.
- Check: nothing.
- A draw offered to him: "Yes." or "Not yet."
- A question about the position: "That is in 1958." "He played it differently." "I do not know this one."
- A question about himself: "I am from Brise." "I came in 1949." "I wind them."
- A question about the Keeper: he looks at the board. If pressed: "The ledgers are in the cabinet."
- The visitor leaves mid-game: "The position keeps."

If a build permits the visitor to take black, Mr Halm takes white without remark, as the ledger records for the forty-one games in which the Keeper took black.

## 7. The set

Item 1-10. Chess set, boxwood and Macassar ebony, 31 pieces and 1 replacement, with board, 45 cm. D. Pending.

Carved 1938 to 1939 by Keeper Gregor Ostrow from crate 1931/4, seized for undeclared duty and forfeited to the Republic after seven years unclaimed. He requisitioned it without authority; the set is therefore Disputed and Pending. The board is of the same woods, file letters and rank numbers stamped into the border with the post's own brass type.

The silhouettes are lathe-turned bodies with hand-carved heads, each head an object of a frontier post, not a person. No piece has a face. King: the standing lamp of the declarations desk, with its glass shade. Queen: the desk's inkstand, lidded. Bishop: the customs seal press, its round head slotted for the die. Knight: the horse of the ferry that preceded the bridge, head down, bridled. Rook: a tower with a lookout window. Pawn: a frontier boundary stone, a short post with a bevelled cap and a numeral, white marked V, black marked H.

The dossiers, item 6-09, are the roster of the Marle Chess Club: typed 1962 to 1964, pencil additions in an adult hand to 1977. Clicking a piece raises its card. Dossiers never attribute feelings to pieces.

**White King, e1. "The Lamp."** Boxwood, 96 mm. Has not left this house since 1939. Mated 211 times as of this card; 340 in pencil.

**White Queen, d1. "Inkstand."** Boxwood, 88 mm. The lid is carved shut. Exchanged for her opposite number 402 times as of this card; 617 in pencil.

**White Rooks, a1 and h1. "Home Tower."** Boxwood, 70 mm. Modelled on this house. The window faces the bridge on both.

**White Bishops, c1 and f1. "Seal."** Boxwood, 78 mm. The die slot on the king's bishop is cut 2 mm deeper. Nobody knows why. In pencil: the maker did.

**White Knights, b1 and g1. "Ferry."** Boxwood, 74 mm; and pearwood, 1972, carved by A. Ostrow to the original pattern. Does not match its partner. The original is on loan.

**White Pawns, a2 to h2. "Stone 1" to "Stone 8."** Boxwood, 48 mm, marked V. Stone 4 is the most frequently moved piece in the house. Promoted twice, 1957 and 1971, both times by Mr Halm's opponent.

**Black King, e8. "Lamp, East."** Ebony, 96 mm. Modelled on the Helder desk lamp, which had a tin shade. Mated 198 times as of this card; 331 in pencil.

**Black Queen, d8. "Inkstand, East."** Ebony, 88 mm. The lid is carved open. In pencil: Mr Halm says it was.

**Black Rooks, a8 and h8. "East Tower."** Ebony, 70 mm. Modelled on the Helder post. In pencil: now the only record of its roofline.

**Black Bishops, c8 and f8. "Seal, Helder."** Ebony, 78 mm. The Helder die was square. The slot is square.

**Black Knights, b8 and g8. "Ferry."** Ebony, 74 mm. The same horse as white, from the same ferry. It carried both ways.

**Black Pawns, a7 to h7. "Stone 1" to "Stone 8."** Ebony, 48 mm, marked H. Stone 7 has a chip in the cap, 1952. Mr Halm dropped it. It is recorded.

## 9. Room one, the chess room

**Frame.** Square to the north wall of Room 1, at the height of a seated man's eye, the desk parallel to the camera. The stage is 16:9. The room fills the left 70 per cent (the scene); the right 30 per cent is the form (the page). Two inserts are permitted: the board, overhead, orthogonal, while play is on; and the tray, overhead, for two seconds when a piece is placed in it.

**Palette.** Six colours, each carried by an object, and no others, including hover and focus states.

| Name | Hex | Carries |
|---|---|---|
| Service Green No. 3 | #6E7D69 | wall distemper; the greatcoat |
| Marle Oak, oiled | #5A4530 | desk, floorboards, door frames, chairs |
| Boxwood | #D8BE8C | light squares, white pieces, index cards, the page ground |
| Macassar Ebony | #2B1F19 | dark squares, black pieces, the stove |
| Seal Wax | #A0281E | the NIL on the Tariff Board, the tray stencil, the DETAINED stamp, chapter-card ground |
| Iron Gall | #202834 | all typescript, clock hands, every outline |

**Rendering.** Uniform outline in Iron Gall, flat fills, no gradient, one flat shadow per object cast straight down. ## 10. Laws of the house

House rules and design constraints, binding on writers, artists and developers.

1. Everything in the house has a number. What has no number is not in the house. Numbers do not collide.
2. A room is seen square to its wall, at the height of a seated man. Nothing is shown from an angle it was not built to be seen from. The board and the tray were built to be seen from above.
3. A feeling is given one sentence. The object is given as many as it needs.
4. Nothing here is called sad. Things are worn, empty, replaced, exempt or pending.
5. Every door has a caption. A locked door has a longer one, and the reason is one the Ministry would accept.
6. The absent are referred to by their post, never by what became of them.
7. Silence is a permitted move. The narrator does not fill it. The column shows a dash.
8. A rule that has outlived its purpose is kept, and nobody remarks on it. This is the chief rule of the house.
9. Everything here was made by hand and says so. Where a machine made it, the machine is named.
10. No colour enters a room without an object to carry it. Six colours to a room.
11. The narrator keeps to the wall and does not touch the pieces. He describes, cites and declines. He does not conclude.
12. Politeness is maintained under all conditions, including checkmate. It is shown, not annotated.
